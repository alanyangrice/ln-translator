## 16. Character voice differentiation
**Sendai:** Sendai’s dialogue is smoother, more teasing, and more socially agile than Miyagi’s. She uses playful pressure—“Come on,” “Wow,” “Sure, sure,” “You’re so stingy”—and often turns Miyagi’s words back on her. In narration, she processes emotion through analysis, social comparison, and practical consequences: Umina’s mood, her parents’ expectations, the money in the piggy bank, or whether something violates a rule.

Sendai notices atmosphere and performance: who is popular, who needs appeasing, what image she is maintaining, and when Miyagi’s behavior deviates from the pattern. She intellectualizes discomfort, but her irritation comes out in sharper, more verbal ways than Miyagi’s. Her prose is more externally oriented and explanatory, while her dialogue can be teasing enough to mask genuine vulnerability.

**Miyagi:** Miyagi’s dialogue is clipped, imperative, and resistant: “No,” “Lick it,” “Come here,” “Be quiet,” “I don’t need it.” She often refuses to explain herself, answers questions with blunt denials, and uses orders to avoid admitting desire or anxiety. In narration, she fixates on whether Sendai is obeying, whether something counts as violence, whether they are friends, and whether Sendai might leave.

Miyagi processes emotion physically before she names it: tightness in the chest, heat, nausea, trembling, restlessness, or the need to touch a fading mark. Her worldview is more territorial and defensive than Sendai’s, with repeated emphasis on “my room,” “my orders,” and the money as the only acceptable connection. Her prose is more circular and denial-heavy, especially around loneliness, jealousy, and attachment.
