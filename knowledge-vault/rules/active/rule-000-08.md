# Rule

Disambiguate high-frequency Japanese verbs by context. When a JP verb has multiple distinct senses, choose the English verb that matches the *specific contextual sense* the scene demands, not the most common dictionary gloss. Falling back to the first dictionary meaning produces calques that are grammatically valid but obviously translated.

The failure mode this rule prevents: the model defaulting to a sense like "call" for 呼ぶ or "cut" for 切る in every occurrence, even when the surrounding context (someone visiting in person, a phone call ending, an intercom being silenced) clearly demands a different English verb.

## Why this is its own rule

This pattern recurs constantly because Japanese has fewer common verbs than English does for the same conceptual space — one JP verb often covers what English splits across three or four. Without explicit disambiguation, the translator overfits to the most-frequent sense seen in training data.

## Examples (grow this list as new verbs surface)

- **呼ぶ** in invitation/visit contexts → "ask her over" / "invite her" / "tell her to come over", **not** "call" or "summon". (Sense: invite to come; not phone call, not magical summoning.)
- **切る** for calls / intercoms → "hang up" / "end the call", **not** "cut off" / "cut the intercom off". (Sense: terminate a connection; not literal cutting.)
- *(Verbs to watch for as the corpus expands: 出る (exit/answer/appear/leave), 入る (enter/fit/soak), 上げる (raise/give/finish), 通る (pass/be accepted/go through), 立つ (stand/be set up/depart). Add concrete examples here when deviations surface.)*

## How to apply

1. When translating a high-frequency verb, identify which English sense is in play from the surrounding sentence and scene.
2. If the literal/first-gloss English verb would feel calque-y when read aloud (e.g. "cut the intercom"), it almost certainly is — pick the contextually idiomatic verb instead.
3. When in doubt, check what the reference window does with the same verb in similar contexts.
