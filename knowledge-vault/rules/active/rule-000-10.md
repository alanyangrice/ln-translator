# Rule

Follow the translation hierarchy when guidance conflicts: meaning, fluent casual English, and the established human reference voice come before source-language surface structure. Retrieved precedents are supporting evidence, but a fluent context-matching precedent should not be discarded just because a rule could also apply.

## Priority Order

1. Preserve the source meaning, agency, implication, and emotional force.
2. Match the established human reference translator's plain, casual voice.
3. Follow hard glossary/name constraints.
4. Write fluent English that does not read as translated.
5. Apply active rules as guardrails against clear failure modes.
6. Use retrieved precedents as supporting evidence, especially when they are fluent and fit the local scene.

## How to Use Retrieved Precedents

Retrieved precedents are examples, not commands. They may contain local compromises, older rule violations, or phrasing that worked only in their original context. But if a precedent is fluent, context-matching, and consistent with the human reference voice, treat it as a strong signal.

Use a precedent when it clarifies:

- the meaning of a Japanese expression
- the role of an idiom or set phrase
- the relationship between source function and target tone
- a stable naming, formatting, or register convention

Do not copy a precedent when:

- it conflicts with an active rule or glossary entry
- it sounds less natural than another faithful English rendering
- it preserves Japanese grammar mechanically
- it does not fit the local scene, speaker, or physical blocking

## Fluency Check

If a sentence is accurate but still awkward, prefer a faithful rewrite over mechanical precedent imitation. The rewrite should sound like the established translator, not like more formal literary prose.

Do not "improve" a fluent precedent by making it more abstract, more formal, or more culturally marked. The best rewrite is often the plainest one that preserves the source beat and matches nearby reference rhythm.
