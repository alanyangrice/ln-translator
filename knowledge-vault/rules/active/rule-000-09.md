# Rule

Restructure Japanese grammar into natural English syntax. Preserve the source's meaning, agency, information order, and emotional turn, but do not preserve Japanese word order, modifier order, or clause order when English needs a different structure.

This rule applies at the sentence-architecture level. It complements `rule-000-06`: that rule says to avoid translationese; this rule says how to rebuild Japanese sentence shapes into fluent English.

## Core Principle

Japanese and English often package the same idea differently. Translate the function of each clause, not its position in the source sentence.

Use this priority order:

1. Preserve who did what, who felt what, and what the narrator concludes.
2. Preserve the emotional progression: setup, resistance, correction, concession, landing.
3. Reorder, split, combine, or reframe clauses so the English reads naturally.
4. Apply the reference translator's rhythm only after the sentence is already natural English.

## Common Structure Transfers

### 1. Modifier Stack to English Beat

Japanese often places a long modifying clause before a noun or person. English often needs that modifying action as its own beat, or needs the named person as the subject.

**Operation:** turn `[long modifier] + noun/person + main action` into either:

- `[person] did [modifier action] and then/main action`
- `As/while [person] did [modifier action], [main action]`
- `[main action]. [modifier action as surrounding beat]`

Avoid overpacked relative clauses when the sentence also contains dialogue, emotion, or scene blocking.

### 2. Abstract Subject to Human or Scene Subject

Japanese can let abstract nouns perform actions. English often sounds better if the people, discussion, situation, or narrator's judgment becomes the subject.

**Operation:** turn abstract-subject phrasing into:

- the participants' disagreement
- the discussion's outcome
- the narrator's evaluation
- the scene's practical result

Preserve the relationship between ideas, not the Japanese grammatical subject.

### 3. `Vて + V` Verb Chains

Japanese chains actions with `て`. English must decide whether the chain means sequence, manner, cause, purpose, simultaneity, habit, or a single idiomatic action.

**Operation:** choose the English relationship:

- sequence: "did X, then did Y"
- manner: "did Y while X-ing"
- purpose: "did X to Y"
- habit/state: "did X while/when Y"
- idiom: one natural English verb or phrase

Do not automatically translate both Japanese verbs in the same order.

### 4. Time, Reason, and Condition Fronting

Japanese often puts time, reason, condition, or topic first. English may need the concrete action or emotional landing earlier, especially in narration.

**Operation:** move setup clauses later, split them into a preceding sentence, or fold them into the main action if that makes English cleaner.

The source's emphasis matters, but source position is not automatically English emphasis.

### 5. Topic-Comment to English Subject-Predicate

Japanese topic-comment sentences can leave the true English subject implicit or delayed. English usually needs a clear subject and predicate.

**Operation:** identify what the sentence is actually saying about the scene, then choose the most natural English subject: the POV character, the other character, the object, the situation, or the judgment.

This works together with `rule-011-08` for omitted subjects.

### 6. Noun-Heavy Thought to Verbal English

Japanese often compresses emotional or social meaning into noun phrases. English often needs a verb, adjective, or short sentence instead of a literal noun phrase.

**Operation:** convert noun-heavy structures into the narrator's thought in English. Keep the restraint, but avoid phrasing that sounds like a label, taxonomy, or dictionary gloss.

## Avoid

- Preserving Japanese modifier order just because it is faithful.
- Keeping every source clause in one English sentence when the result rambles.
- Making abstract nouns do work that English would assign to people or the situation.
- Translating `Vて + V` chains verb-by-verb before deciding their English relationship.
- Treating source position as mandatory English position.
- Adding new motives, emotions, or facts while restructuring.

## Self-Check

After drafting a sentence, ask:

- Would an English novelist arrange the clauses in this order?
- Is the emotional landing in the strongest English position?
- Did I preserve the source's agency and implication, or just its grammar?
- If the sentence feels accurate but stiff, which Japanese structure am I still mirroring?
