# Rule

Render the source idiomatically rather than calquing the surface form. When a Japanese noun phrase, idiom, cooking term, or grammatical construction has no natural English word-for-word equivalent, use what a fluent English novelist would actually write rather than mirroring the Japanese structure. A phrase that is "technically correct English" but that no real English speaker would use in this context is a failure of this rule. Read the candidate sentence aloud — if a native English reader would think "this sounds translated," try harder.

## Two levels of failure to watch for

This rule covers **both** of:

1. **First-order calques** — a single word or phrase is obviously the wrong English (`karaoke box`, `summon`, `cut the intercom off`). These are easy to spot.
2. **Second-order translationese** — the sentence is grammatically valid English but reads as translated, usually because it *mirrors a JP structural pattern* rather than reaching for the equivalent English construction. These are subtle, recur across chapters, and survive past the obvious calques.

Both levels matter. The second-order patterns below are what separate "passes the rule check" from "reads like a published English novel."

## First-order calques (single words / phrases)

- カラオケボックス → "karaoke booth" or just "karaoke", NOT "karaoke box".
- 千切り → "thinly slice (the cabbage)" / "shred", NOT "do julienne" or "julienne the cabbage".
- 部屋の主 → "Miyagi" / "the girl who actually lived there", NOT "the homeowner".
- いつメン → "the girls in our group" / "our usual crowd", NOT "our usual members".
- 過去の前例に倣って → "going by how things had gone before" / "based on past behavior", NOT "true to past precedent of".
- 部活帰り → "after club" / "on the way home from club", NOT "club returnings" or "at club return".
- 自販機 → "vending machine"; do not strip context that makes it natural ("grabbed a drink from the vending machine", not bare "the self-vending machine").

## Second-order translationese (structural patterns)

Each of the nine patterns below has surfaced multiple times in chapter audits. When you spot one of these *shapes* in your draft, apply the fix pattern; don't translate verb-by-verb.

### 1. Bare-noun → definite-article calque

JP uses bare topical nouns where EN demands a possessive, a pronoun, or a rephrase. Translating these as "the X" produces sentences that sound clinical and disembodied.

- 初めて触れたときよりも素直に応えてくれた体 → "Her body, more honest in answering me than the first time I'd touched her." **NOT** "The body that had responded to me more honestly than it had the first time…"
- まかない → "dinner at the cafe" / "they feed us at work". **NOT** "the staff meal" (`the` + foreign concept reads jarringly clinical).

**Fix pattern**: replace `the [body part / object]` with a possessive (`her body / my chest / your shirt`) or rephrase entirely. A bare definite article on a sentence-final JP topical noun is almost always a calque.

### 2. Two-verb action beats compressed in JP

JP routinely uses terse two-verb sequences for routine actions (息を吸って吐く, 立ち上がって座る). EN compresses these into a single idiomatic verb. Translating verb-by-verb over-describes the action.

- 息を吸って吐く → "I took a deep breath and let it go." / "I breathed in slowly and let it out." **NOT** "I took a breath in, then let it out." (mechanical sequence rather than a single beat).

**Fix pattern**: don't render the JP action sequence verb-by-verb. Find the single EN verb (or single beat) that captures the whole action.

### 3. Body-part push/grab constructions

JP body-part constructions (肩を押される, 肩を押す, 髪を掴まれる, 手を取られる) don't transfer mechanically to "[verb] me/her [by/at] the [body part]". The body part can be named when it is physically important, but the English should describe the action naturally, not preserve the JP `body-part-を-VERBED` structure.

- 肩を押された → "she shoved me back" / "I felt her hands on my shoulders, pushing me back." **NOT** "she pushed me away by the shoulders" (mirrors JP body-part-を structure).
- 思わず仙台さんの肩を押す → "I shoved Sendai-san back before I could think" / "I pushed against Sendai-san's shoulders without thinking." **NOT** "I pushed her away by the shoulders" when the result sounds like a body-part calque.

**Fix pattern**: rewrite around what's being done to whom — what the body part receives, not the JP `body-part-を-VERBED` structure.

### 4. Mirrored intensifier constructions

JP intensifiers (呆れるほど, 痛いほど, 死ぬほど) don't transfer literally. The EN equivalent is an adverb or pre-modifier, not a `enough to be X` clause.

- 呆れるほど強く噛み → "an absurdly hard bite" / "ridiculously hard". **NOT** "hard enough to be ridiculous."
- 死ぬほど嬉しい → "ecstatic" / "thrilled beyond words". **NOT** "happy enough to die."
- 痛いほど (eye contact / silence / etc.) → "almost painfully [intense / quiet]". **NOT** "painful enough to hurt."

**Fix pattern**: use the EN equivalent intensifier (`absurdly`, `ridiculously`, `painfully`, `beyond words`) as an adverb or pre-modifier, not as a `enough to be X` clause.

### 5. Aspect/tense drift in interior narration

JP narration often slips into present tense for interior-monologue moments even inside past-tense sequences. EN requires consistent aspect; a present-tense relative clause inside past-tense narration reads as a tense slip.

- (past-tense narration) よからぬことが頭に浮かぶ → "thoughts I had no business having surfaced" / "thoughts I shouldn't have been having surfaced". **NOT** "thoughts I shouldn't be having surfaced" (present-tense `shouldn't be having` clashes with past-tense `surfaced`).

**Fix pattern**: if the surrounding narration is past tense, conjugate relative clauses to match (`had no business having` / `shouldn't have been having`), not present-tense (`shouldn't be having`).

### 6. Verb-sense and framing drift

Verbs with subtly different EN counterparts default to the most common gloss instead of the contextually correct one. (This complements `rule-000-08` — that rule covers high-frequency *verb-sense* disambiguation; this section covers connotation-level drift and inverted framing.)

- 意識する (in relationship contexts) → "be aware of" / "be conscious of". **NOT** "think about" (loses the JP nuance of being *aware of someone romantically*).
- 食材が減っていなかった → "nothing in the fridge had been touched" / "the food in the fridge hadn't been touched." **NOT** "how little had been used from the fridge" (the JP frames it as the food *not decreasing*; the LLM inverts to a quantitative framing).

**Fix pattern**: when a verb has multiple senses, pick the one the scene demands. When the JP framing is negative (`hadn't decreased`, `wasn't said`), preserve the negative framing — don't flip it to a positive quantitative statement.

### 7. Abstract noun / metaphor calques

Japanese often encodes social, emotional, or rhetorical meaning in compact abstract nouns, set phrases, and physical metaphors. Do not preserve the Japanese noun-shape if it produces English that sounds technical, clinical, or translated. Render the function the phrase has in context.

**Fix pattern**: identify what the phrase is doing in the scene, then use the natural English expression for that function. This is allowed under `rule-000-02` when it preserves the source's force and does not add new facts or stronger emotion.

**Avoid**:

- literal abstractions that no English novelist would write
- technical wording for ordinary social judgments
- scientific wording for emotional body sensations
- preserving a metaphor's object when English would preserve its function

Example: a Japanese phrase about social "distance" in a relationship should usually become English about boundaries, personal space, or overfamiliarity, not literal physical distance.

### 8. Time-elapsed setup clauses

JP often opens a sentence with a compact elapsed-time phrase to emphasize how quickly something happened (`会って一分で`, `数秒で`, `一日もしないうちに`). A literal "after X..." opener can sound stiff in English.

**Fix pattern**: keep the same elapsed time and emphasis, but use natural English pacing: "we'd only just...", "barely...", "all of X", "not even X later", etc. Do not add extra duration or intensity; only reshape the time setup.

### 9. Pushing through an interaction

JP phrases like `無理矢理してしまえばいい`, `なんとかする`, or `押し切る` can describe pushing past social resistance to get an action done. Translating them as "force X through" often sounds bureaucratic or physical.

**Fix pattern**: translate the interactional intent: "push ahead and...", "get X done somehow", "steer the conversation back to...", "press on with...", depending on context. Preserve the resistance/awkwardness without inventing aggression.

### 10. Known-speaker voice metonymy

JP often lets a "voice" perform the action (`明るい声が聞こえてくる`, `静かな声が返ってくる`) where English sounds more natural naming the already-known speaker. Do not hide a clear speaker behind "a voice" unless the source is deliberately withholding who spoke.

- 向かい側から明るい声が聞こえてくる → "Sendai-san asked brightly from across the table." **NOT** "I heard a cheerful voice from across from me" when Sendai-san is plainly the speaker.
- 軽やかな声が返ってくる → "Sendai-san answered lightly." / "came Sendai-san's light reply." **NOT** "a light voice came back" if that sounds disembodied.

### 11. Formal speech-act verbs in casual narration

Japanese can use formal action labels like `宣言する` for a small comic beat. English often needs a lighter verb unless the scene is truly formal or emphatic.

- 「食べない」と宣言する → "I said, 'I'm not eating it.'" / "I told her, 'I'm not eating it.'" **NOT** "declared, 'I'm not eating it'" when the line is ordinary Miyagi resistance rather than a theatrical announcement.

### 12. JP relative-clause stacking

Japanese often modifies a named person with a whole action clause before the main verb. A direct English relative clause (`Sendai-san, who had...`) can sound stiff or overpacked, especially when the sentence also contains dialogue. Turn the action into its own beat or make the named person the subject.

**Fix pattern**: if an English relative clause feels overloaded, promote the modifying action into a separate beat or make the named character the subject of the sentence. Keep the same action and timing, but do not preserve the Japanese modifier stack merely because it appears before the main verb.

### 13. Deadlock/personification phrases

When JP assigns an action to abstract nouns like `意見`, English often needs the people or discussion as the subject. Preserve the stalemate, not the abstract noun shape.

**Fix pattern**: when an abstract noun is the grammatical subject in JP, ask whether English would more naturally make the people, the discussion, or the narrator's judgment the subject. Preserve the stalemate or evaluation, not the Japanese noun shape.

### 14. Small interior-voice noun phrases

Compact JP noun phrases in monologue should sound like the narrator's thought, not a dictionary gloss.

**Fix pattern**: choose an English phrase that sounds like the POV character thinking in the moment. Avoid dictionary-shaped noun phrases, forced perfect aspect, or school-essay abstractions. The right answer depends on tense, judgment, and rhythm.

## Self-check

Read the candidate sentence aloud. Two failure modes:

1. **Obvious calque** — a single word or phrase is wrong. Catch via the first-order examples above.
2. **Subtle translationese** — the sentence is grammatically valid but reads "off" to a native ear. Almost always one of the structural patterns above. Catch by asking: *would a published English novelist write this exact sentence?* If you can't picture it on the page of a published novel, rewrite.
